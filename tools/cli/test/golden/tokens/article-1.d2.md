# The Quiet Return of Rooftop Beekeeping

Across the old harbour district, a handful of hobbyists are turning flat tar roofs into small, humming farms.

By [Marta Quillfeather](/staff/mquillfeather) March 14, 2026 6 min read

*   [Share on a feed](#)
*   [Send by mail](#)
*   [Copy link](#)

![Three white hive boxes on a gravel roof]()

Three hive boxes sit beside a chimney on a warehouse roof near the fish market.

On a windless morning in late spring, Teodor Ashgrove climbs a service ladder with a smoker in one hand and a thermos in the other. The roof above the old rope works is warm already, and the air carries a faint smell of clover from the allotments two streets away.

Ashgrove keeps four colonies here. He began with one, bought from a retired signalman who had grown tired of the stairs. "I assumed the wind would be the problem," he says, lifting a lid to show frames heavy with capped honey. "It turned out the bees do not mind wind at all. They mind neighbours who panic."

## A craft with a long memory

Rooftop hives are not new to the district. Ledgers kept by the harbour guild list a dozen apiaries on warehouse roofs a century ago, each paying a token rent in wax candles. The practice faded when the warehouses emptied, and was largely forgotten until a small group of volunteers revived it three summers ago.

The volunteers meet on the first Sunday of each month in a converted loading bay. They trade swarm boxes, argue about smoke, and keep a shared logbook of weather, nectar flow and the occasional stung ankle. The newest members are often students from the nearby design college, who treat the hives as a puzzle in ventilation.

> A roof is a strange pasture, but it is an honest one. There is nothing between the bees and the sky.

## What the honey tastes like

Tasters who have sampled the first harvest describe it as dark, slightly smoky and unexpectedly floral. Lime trees along the quay account for much of the flavour, with a trace of heather blown in from the hills. A small batch sold out at the spring market within an hour, at a price that surprised even the keepers.

Not everyone is convinced. A pest-control firm has warned that crowded roofs could encourage disease between colonies, and the city council is drafting a short registry so that apiaries can be tracked. Ashgrove welcomes it. "If something goes wrong, I would like to be the first to know," he says.

For now the volunteers are planning a modest expansion: two more roofs, a shared extraction room, and a weekend course for beginners. The first class has already filled, and the waiting list stretches well into autumn.
