Notes on Slow Software

Posted by Idris Calloway · 9 January 2026 · 11 minute read

On this page
  1. Why slow
  2. Small batches
  3. Tools I keep
  4. Closing thought

For most of a decade I measured my work in how quickly a change reached strangers. A faster pipeline felt like a better pipeline, and a week without a release felt like a small failure. This essay is about why I stopped counting that way.

Why slow

Slow does not mean idle. It means that every step has room to be understood before the next one begins. When a team compresses design, building and review into a single afternoon, the compression has to land somewhere, and it usually lands on the person who maintains the result a year later.

I noticed the pattern first in my own repositories. The files I enjoyed returning to were rarely the ones I wrote quickly. They were the ones where I had paused, deleted a clever abstraction, and left a plain function with an honest name.

Small batches

The practical habit that changed things was shrinking the unit of work. Instead of a branch that lived for two weeks, I kept changes small enough to explain in a single paragraph. If I could not describe the change plainly, it was not ready to be written.

Small batches also made disagreement cheaper. A reviewer can accept or reject a ten-line change in the time it takes to drink a cup of tea, and nobody has to defend a month of work in a single meeting.

Tools I keep

My toolbox has shrunk. A plain text editor, a terminal, a notebook with a soft pencil, and a calendar with long empty stretches. I have tried more elaborate setups, and each one eventually asked me to serve it rather than the other way around.

  • A single script that builds the site in under a second.
  • A weekly review where I delete more than I add.
  • A rule that no tool is adopted before its predecessor is retired.

Closing thought

None of this is an argument against speed where speed is cheap. It is an argument for noticing what the speed is costing, and for being willing, now and then, to let a good idea sit until it stops being merely exciting.
